# Fatewalker Lore Bible
## Internal canon and narrative rules

This document is the continuity reference for Fatewalker. It describes what the world currently believes, what is known to be true, and what must remain mysterious until the player earns the answer. Future quests and dialogue should build on this canon rather than casually contradicting it.

## The central premise

Fatewalker is a story about memory, responsibility, and whether a life can change the meaning of another life. Rebirth is real, but it is not a clean reset. A soul carries fragments forward: oaths, scars, habits, relics, fears, loyalties, and memories that may be incomplete or misinterpreted. These remnants influence later eras.

The player's black thread is not simply a mark of divine favor. It is an unresolved consequence made visible: a choice from an earlier cycle has become entangled with choices the player has not made yet. The thread responds to places and objects connected to that unresolved event.

The core mystery should not be answered early: **why does this soul keep returning, and what happened at the first shore?** Several people have partial evidence. None has the whole truth.

## The river and the Underworld

The Styx is not merely a river separating life from death. It is a boundary that gives shape to what a soul is willing—or able—to remember. It can blur a name, distort a chronology, or leave a memory intact while removing the context that made it understandable. The river is powerful, but it is not omniscient and does not always distinguish voluntary surrender from a wound.

Charon ferries the dead; he is not their judge. He refuses to describe the river as good or evil. His work has taught him that wealth, status, and declarations of innocence mean little at the crossing. He respects honest responsibility more than heroic self-description.

Asphodel is a place of unfinished understanding, not simply a punishment for unremarkable souls. Some shades remain because their lives were ordinary; others remain because memory has been damaged so thoroughly that they cannot make sense of their own choices. The Unremembered Shade is one such witness. Its clay token carries three marks: a straight line, a broken line, and an unfinished line. The marks recur in Asterion and may eventually be tied to the first crossing.

Cerberus guards a threshold. The three heads are traditionally described as three kinds of fear: the fear admitted, the fear disguised as anger, and the fear mistaken for courage. He is not a random monster placed before a boss door. His role is to prevent unresolved truths from returning unchanged. The player must eventually learn why the gate needs guarding.

Tartarus is older than the current order of Olympus. It is not merely a deep dungeon, but a place where the consequences the gods cannot settle are confined. The game should reveal this gradually through evidence, testimony, architecture, and choices—not a single exposition dump.

## The gods, the Fates, and choice

The gods are not a unified moral authority. They have competing needs, histories, loyalties, and blind spots. A divine gift can be useful and still create an obligation. NPCs should not treat every divine act as unquestionably good or every act of defiance as automatically noble.

The Fates (Moirai) are interpreted differently by different people. Some believe they determine every outcome; others believe they measure what choices make possible. The game should preserve tension between pattern and agency. Prophecy can identify pressure, danger, or likely consequence without making the player's decisions meaningless.

Pythia's central warning is that a prophecy is not permission. Trusting her means agreeing to remember the warning and accept responsibility for how it is used; it does not mean surrendering free will. Defying her means refusing her offered interpretation, not escaping consequence. Both paths must remain narratively valid.

## The black thread and the first shore

Asterion's old well reflects a shore that does not belong to the present. The village has inherited customs whose origins have been forgotten: empty bowls for the absent, offerings of bread and salt, and a refusal to answer a voice from the well with one's name. These are folk protections built around real events, although the villagers no longer agree on the explanation.

The three-line symbol appears on the milestone, the shade's clay token, and in later evidence. Its meaning is not yet fully known. Do not explain it away as a simple map marker or a generic symbol of fate. It should connect the player's present choices to a forgotten act of departure, betrayal, or mercy.

The first shore is a future story destination and a conceptual anchor: a place where memory survives without being made into a flattering legend. The eventual revelation should complicate the player's understanding of their earlier lives rather than simply reveal that they were secretly a famous hero.

## Eras and continuity

### Ancient Greece
Asterion is a small village beneath Olympus. The mountain's creatures are behaving with unusual intent: the Harpy steals letters and keepsakes rather than food or coin; other monsters leave signs around sealed shrines. Delphi can sense the player's thread but cannot identify its origin. Theron treats monster remains as dangerous memories that can be shaped into gear.

### The Underworld
Charon and the shades offer conflicting testimony about memory and responsibility. The river, Asphodel, judgment, Cerberus, and Tartarus should feel like connected institutions with different purposes. Optional quests deepen the player's understanding and may grant persistent memories, oaths, or echoes.

### Modern Athens
Ancient places have been built over, catalogued, commercialized, and partially forgotten. The city does not simply become a reskin of ancient Greece: cameras, museums, transit maps, and public records become new ways to witness impossible events. Dr. Eleni Vassos approaches the supernatural as an investigator. Myrto knows that the city's old objects have histories that do not fit their labels.

### Future eras
Future lives should change the world's assumptions, not merely its technology. The Lunar Oracle sees multiple skies and warns that different futures may be true in different branches. The long arc concerns whether rebirth repairs an ancient wound or keeps it open.

## Character truths and voice

- **Damon:** A village survivor. He speaks in practical customs, family memories, and hard-won caution. He believes food and hospitality should come before interrogation.
- **Pythia:** Precise, unsettling, and ethically serious. She speaks in images but does not use vagueness as a substitute for thought. She distrusts certainty, including her own.
- **Theron:** A craftsperson who sees material as a record of what happened to it. He respects useful skill and distrusts anyone who calls power free.
- **Charon:** Patient, dry, and unsentimental without being cruel. He has seen every kind of person arrive and refuses to confuse wealth with worth.
- **Unremembered Shade:** Vulnerable but not merely helpless. It asks questions about testimony, ordinary love, and whether judgment can be fair when memory has been damaged.
- **Dr. Eleni Vassos:** A methodical researcher whose skepticism has not disappeared; it has become more demanding. She records evidence and admits when the evidence exceeds her explanations.
- **Myrto:** Wry and streetwise. She understands that the modern city profits from selling history while ignoring what history remembers.
- **The Lunar Oracle:** Speaks from a perspective in which futures can overlap. Her statements should create meaningful questions, not provide convenient answers.

## Writing rules for quests and dialogue

1. **No quest should be only a keyword transaction.** The keyword is the interface; the narrative exchange should contain a concrete detail, a character's motive, and a question or consequence.
2. **Give the player something to interpret.** A repeated symbol, a contradiction, an object, a custom, or a witness account is stronger than an NPC simply saying “go north.”
3. **Use specific sensory details.** Stone, salt, bronze, rain, ash, bread, linen, cold water, traffic, and recorded sound should distinguish places and eras.
4. **Let characters disagree.** Charon's account of the river need not match Pythia's prophecy or Eleni's evidence. Their disagreement should be intentional and traceable.
5. **Choices must have a cost or a remembered consequence.** Not every choice needs a branching dungeon, but the world should respond through flags, later dialogue, soul records, faction standing, item descriptions, or quest outcomes.
6. **Do not make every NPC omniscient.** Each person knows only what they saw, were told, inferred, or misunderstood.
7. **Make optional stories enrich the main mystery.** Side quests should add evidence or emotional meaning without becoming mandatory exposition.
8. **Keep the interface concise outside story moments.** Room descriptions should establish place and mood; look, talk, journal, and quest details can provide more. Avoid printing the same lore paragraph on every command.
9. **Respect player agency.** A hint may point to evidence, but it should not dictate what the player must believe.
10. **Write for replay.** Repeated dialogue may acknowledge a completed event or changed relationship instead of repeating the initial speech word for word.

## Open mysteries to preserve

- What happened at the first shore, and why was the player's year wrong?
- Why does the black thread appear in artifacts and records across centuries?
- What do the three lines mean, and why do the village milestone and shade's token share them?
- Why does the Harpy collect messages and keepsakes?
- What did the player or a previous incarnation refuse to do for the gods?
- What is confined in Tartarus that the Olympians cannot settle?
- Is rebirth a gift, a punishment, a repair mechanism, or a system someone has learned to exploit?

Answers should emerge through multiple independent sources, physical clues, and consequences across eras. Avoid resolving an open mystery in a single NPC monologue.
